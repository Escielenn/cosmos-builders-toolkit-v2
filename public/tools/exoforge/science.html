Showing Our Work

The physics and data behind ExoForge
Inputs and consequences

ExoForge takes five physical inputs: the planet's mass, radius and Bond albedo, its orbital distance, and the star's mass. Everything else follows from those. Temperature and orbital period are no longer sliders, because in a real system they are consequences: move the planet outward and it gets colder and its year gets longer.

Every formula lives in /tools/_shared/physics.js, the same module (twin of src/lib/physics) the rest of StellarForge uses. Unit tests check it against Earth, Mars, Proxima b and TRAPPIST-1e.

The star

The star's mass sets its temperature and radius through the mean main-sequence sequence; its luminosity follows by the Stefan–Boltzmann law, L/L☉ = (R/R☉)² (T/5772 K)⁴. A planet imported from the archive keeps its star's published temperature and radius (a "measured" star, which may not be on the main sequence); if the archive gives no stellar mass, ExoForge estimates one from the temperature and says so.

Pecaut, M.J. & Mamajek, E.E. (2013). ApJS, 208, 9; E. Mamajek, "A Modern Mean Dwarf Stellar Color and Effective Temperature Sequence" (2022).
Orbit, light and temperature
P = 365.25 × √(a³ / (M★ + m_p)) days    S / S⊕ = L / a²
T_eq = T₀ · ((1 − A) / 4)^¼,  T₀ = (S·S⊕ / σ)^¼    T_surface = T_eq · (1 + ¾τ)^¼

The period comes from Kepler's third law with the star's mass. Insolation S is in units of the sunlight Earth receives. The equilibrium temperature assumes heat spread evenly round the planet (Earth: 255 K). The surface temperature adds a grey greenhouse with infrared optical depth τ = 0.84 per Earth atmosphere, which takes Earth from 255 K to 288 K.

The archive also lists an equilibrium temperature for many planets. It rests on the archive's own albedo and redistribution assumptions, so ExoForge shows it beside its own value rather than in place of it.

Cowan, N.B. & Agol, E. (2011). ApJ, 729, 54. Catling, D.C. & Kasting, J.F. (2017). Atmospheric Evolution on Inhabited and Lifeless Worlds, §2.
Habitable zone

The habitable zone is computed for the actual star with the Kopparapu et al. (2014) flux limits, which depend on the star's temperature: In the zone between the runaway-greenhouse and maximum-greenhouse limits, Optimistic out to the recent-Venus and early-Mars limits, otherwise too hot or too cold. The old fixed 180–330 K band is gone.

Kopparapu, R.K. et al. (2014). "Habitable Zones around Main-sequence Stars: Dependence on Planetary Mass." ApJ Letters, 787, L29.
Mass, radius and density
g = M / R²    ρ = 5.514 × M / R³ g/cm³    v_esc = 11.186 × √(M / R) km/s

Mass and radius are both inputs, so ExoForge checks them against each other. The Chen & Kipping (2017) relation gives the typical radius for a mass (rocky below 2 Earth masses, Neptune-like up to about 130, then flat for giants), with its published scatter. A pair more than two standard deviations away reads Unusual. A planet denser than pure iron at its mass (Zeng et al. 2016) reads Implausible, the badge says Impossible density, and a flag names the density and the radius the mass would normally have. One Earth radius at 50 Earth masses, 276 g/cm³, is the example: no material is that dense.

The classification (Terrestrial, Super-Earth, Mini-Neptune, Neptune-like, Gas giant) comes from this mass–radius regime, not from the appearance slider.

Chen, J. & Kipping, D. (2017). "Probabilistic Forecasting of the Masses and Radii of Other Worlds." ApJ, 834, 17. Zeng, L., Sasselov, D.D. & Jacobsen, S.B. (2016). ApJ, 819, 127.
Air retention and water

A gas is held for billions of years if its molecules' mean thermal speed at the top of the atmosphere is at most a sixth of the escape velocity. ExoForge tests nitrogen for rocky looks and hydrogen for gaseous ones, at an assumed 1000 K exobase. Liquid water is Possible on a rocky, plausible planet whose surface temperature lies between freezing and the boiling point at its pressure.

Importing real planets

A set of planets from the NASA Exoplanet Archive (pscomppars) is built in. A search that finds nothing there queries the archive's public TAP service directly from your browser; if the browser or network refuses, ExoForge says so. There is no server-side proxy.

Missing values are never filled silently. A missing mass is estimated from the radius with Chen & Kipping (2017); a giant planet's mass cannot be recovered from its radius, so a Jupiter mass is assumed and labelled. A missing stellar mass is estimated from the star's temperature. Every estimate and assumption (including the Bond albedo, which the archive does not list) is listed under Source in the readout and saved with the planet. Rings are never added at random.

NASA Exoplanet Archive. Caltech/IPAC. exoplanetarchive.ipac.caltech.edu
Appearance

The look slider (rocky to gaseous), ocean and cloud cover, roughness, rings and spin change the picture only. Terrain is fractal Simplex noise; gas giants switch to latitude bands; the atmosphere is a rim-glow shader; the star's light takes its blackbody colour. The surface temperature drives the terrain palette, so moving the orbit recolours the world. Spin stops when your system asks for reduced motion.

Perlin, K. (1985). ACM SIGGRAPH Computer Graphics, 19(3), 287. Sudarsky, D., Burrows, A. & Pinto, P. (2000). ApJ, 538, 885.
What this tool cannot do

No climate model. One equilibrium temperature and one greenhouse number, no circulation, day–night contrast or seasons. For a tidally locked world, use Tidelock.

No geology. Terrain is procedural, not tectonic.

No interior model. The mass–radius check is a statistical relation and an iron floor, not a structure calculation. It catches impossible worlds; it cannot tell you what an unusual one is made of.

Where ExoForge assumes a value (greenhouse per atmosphere, exobase temperature, an archive gap), the readout or this page says so.
For writers

Set the star and the orbit, then read what follows: the year, the light, the temperature and whether the mass and radius you chose can exist. Publish the planet and those numbers become facts your scenes can cite.
