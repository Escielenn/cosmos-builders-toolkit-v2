Showing Our Work

EXOSKY is not a painting. Every star, every twist of the Milky Way, every atmospheric hue is computed from real astrophysical data and peer-reviewed models. Here's how we built an alien night sky you can trust — and what "trust" means when we're extrapolating beyond what any human has ever seen.

METHODOLOGY
One Big Lie, Everything Else Earned

Science fiction worldbuilding at StellarForge follows a single principle: break one law of physics if you must, then rigorously compute the consequences. EXOSKY's "lie" is modest — we're showing you a sky you can't yet visit. But the sky itself is honest. Every photon's journey from source star to alien retina follows the same physics it would in reality.

LAYER 1
The Star Catalog

EXOSKY begins with ~300 named stars drawn from the ESA Hipparcos astrometric catalog — the same dataset that underpins modern stellar astronomy. For each star, we carry five fundamental parameters:

PER-STAR PARAMETERS
Right Ascension (α)Equatorial J2000 coordinates (degrees)
Declination (δ)Equatorial J2000 coordinates (degrees)
DistanceHipparcos parallax → parsecs
Absolute Magnitude (M)Intrinsic luminosity on magnitude scale
B-V Color IndexSpectral energy → surface temperature

These are real, measured values — not artistic choices. Sirius sits at 2.64 pc with absolute magnitude −1.43 because that's what the parallax measurements say. Betelgeuse glows at B-V 1.85 because its photosphere really is that red.

The B-V color index is converted to screen RGB using a physically-motivated piecewise function that maps the color-temperature relationship through five spectral regimes: hot blue (B-V < 0), blue-white (0–0.4), white-yellow (0.4–0.8), orange (0.8–1.2), and deep red (>1.2).

LAYER 2
Procedural Galactic Stars

300 named stars don't fill a sky. The real Milky Way contains ~100–400 billion stars, and even from Earth, thousands are visible to the naked eye. EXOSKY generates ~25,000 procedural stars that obey the same galactic structure as real ones.

Each procedural star is placed in 3D galactocentric coordinates using a multi-step process: random distance (3–8,000 pc, weighted toward nearby), random galactic longitude, and galactic latitude drawn from a Gaussian matching the thin disk's scale height of 300 pc. Stars are then tested against the spiral arm model — those falling between arms are stochastically rejected at higher rates, creating realistic density contrast.

LUMINOSITY DISTRIBUTION
Red dwarfs / dim main-sequence40%
Mid main-sequence (Sun-like)25%
Bright main-sequence / subgiants20%
Giants10%
Supergiants / hypergiants5%

A third layer — 20,000 background field stars — adds the "powdered sugar" effect of unresolved stellar light. These are concentrated along the galactic plane (50% within ±8° galactic latitude, Gaussian distribution) with a density boost toward the galactic center, producing the characteristic brightening when you look toward Sagittarius.

LAYER 3
Coordinate Transforms

This is where the alien perspective happens. To see the sky from Proxima Centauri b instead of Earth, we need to physically move the observer through 3D space and recompute every star's position.

// Step 1: Star's position in heliocentric equatorial XYZ
x = d · cos(δ) · cos(α)
y = d · cos(δ) · sin(α)
z = d · sin(δ)

// Step 2: Observer's position in the same frame
obs = raDecDistToXYZ(planet.ra, planet.dec, planet.dist)

// Step 3: New relative position
Δx = star.x − obs.x, Δy = star.y − obs.y, Δz = star.z − obs.z

// Step 4: New RA, Dec, Distance from observer
new_dist = √(Δx² + Δy² + Δz²)
new_dec = arcsin(Δz / new_dist)
new_ra = atan2(Δy, Δx)

// Step 5: Recalculate apparent magnitude from new distance
m = M + 5 · log₁₀(new_dist / 10)

This is the distance modulus equation — the same formula astronomers use to convert between intrinsic brightness and observed brightness. A star that's magnitude 1 from Earth might be magnitude −2 (blazingly bright) from a planet 10× closer, or magnitude 8 (invisible to the naked eye) from a planet 100× farther.

The result is that familiar constellations distort. From Proxima Centauri (4.2 ly away), Orion is barely changed — those stars are hundreds of light-years distant, so a 4 ly shift barely matters. But from Kepler-442b (1,100 ly away), the entire sky is unrecognizable. The constellation drawer exists precisely for this: to name what you see from there.

LAYER 3B
Equatorial ↔ Galactic Frame

The Milky Way doesn't align with Earth's equator. Converting between the equatorial coordinate system (RA/Dec, oriented to Earth's spin axis) and the galactic system (oriented to the Milky Way's disk plane) requires a 3×3 rotation matrix defined by the IAU 1958 galactic coordinate standard:

IAU GALACTIC COORDINATE SYSTEM
North Galactic Pole (J2000)α = 192.859°, δ = 27.128°
Galactic Center directionl = 0°, b = 0° → Sagittarius A*
Ascending node (l of NCP)122.932°

The observer's position is then converted to galactocentric coordinates by offsetting from the Sun's known position: R = 8,200 pc from the galactic center, z = 25 pc above the plane. This positions the observer correctly within the galaxy's structure for Milky Way rendering.

LAYER 3C
Axial Precession

A planet's rotational axis does not point at a fixed spot among the stars forever. Gravity from a sun and moon tugs on the bulge of a spinning, tilted world, and the axis slowly traces a cone, once every roughly 25,772 years for Earth. This is why Polaris was not the pole star when the pyramids were built (Thuban held that spot then), and why Vega will take over the job around 13,727 CE. The EPOCH slider lets you turn that same wheel for any of EXOSKY's observation points, up to a half-cycle in either direction.

// Precession angle, one full cycle every 25,772 years
θ = −2π · (yearsFromNow / 25772)

// Rotate into the ecliptic frame (tilted 23.4393° from the equator),
// spin by θ around the ecliptic pole, rotate back out
v_epoch = R_ecliptic⁻¹ · R_z(θ) · R_ecliptic · v_now

This is applied as a single rotation matrix to every named and procedural star's observer-frame position before it's converted back to RA/Dec, and to the Milky Way band's own sight-line direction before its galactic-coordinate lookup, so the structured sky, stars and band together, turns as one, the way a real precessing sky does, rather than each star drifting independently. It is a pure rotation: distances and relative geometry between stars are unchanged, only which direction is "up" relative to the fixed stars. The unresolved 20,000-star background field (Layer 2's "powdered sugar" layer) is a separate, static procedural texture and does not yet share this rotation.

LAYER 4
Milky Way Structure

The Milky Way band is not a texture map or a photograph — it's volumetrically ray-marched from the observer's position through a 3D model of the galaxy. Each direction on the sky is traced through interstellar space, accumulating stellar density and dust extinction along the way.

GALACTIC STRUCTURE MODEL
Disk radial scale length2,600 pc
Thin disk scale height300 pc
Dust lane scale height120 pc
Galactic bulge radius1,200 pc
Spiral arm pitch angle12°
Spiral arm Gaussian width500 pc
Ray march distance15,000 pc (40 steps)
Map resolution360 × 180 (1° per cell)

The density model combines three components: an exponential disk (density falls off with distance from center and height above plane), four logarithmic spiral arms following the Milky Way's observed structure, and a central bulge modeled as an oblate Gaussian.

// Exponential disk
ρ_disk = exp(−R / 2600) · exp(−|z| / 300)

// Spiral arm enhancement (4 logarithmic arms)
θ_arm = θ₀ + ln(R / R_ref) / tan(12°)
arm_factor = 1 + 2.5 · exp(−d²_arm / 2σ²)

// Galactic bulge (oblate)
r_bulge = √(R² + 4z²)
ρ_bulge = 3.0 · exp(−r²_bulge / 1200²)

// Dust extinction along ray
τ += ρ_dust · Δs · 0.0001
attenuation = exp(−τ)

Scutum-Centaurus180°1.1×
Norma-Outer270°0.8×

Dust is critical. The dark lanes bisecting the Milky Way exist because interstellar dust absorbs and scatters light. Our dust model follows the same exponential disk with a thinner scale height (120 pc), creating realistic obscuration of distant stars and the characteristic dark rift through Cygnus.

The MW brightness map is pre-computed as a 360 × 180 grid on a background thread, then rendered using bilinear interpolation for smooth gradients. Each screen pixel is reverse-projected to galactic coordinates and sampled from this map, colored with a warm-to-cool gradient (warmer near the galactic center, cooler in the outer disk).

LAYER 5
Atmospheric Optics

An atmosphere transforms a sky. Rayleigh scattering makes Earth's sky blue. CO₂ scattering on Mars makes it butterscotch. EXOSKY models atmosphere effects using three physically-motivated parameters per atmospheric type:

AtmosphereSky ColorExtinctionHazeEffect
No atmosphere[0,0,0]0.000.00Raw starlight, vacuum
Thin N₂[5,8,18]0.080.05Faint indigo wash
Thin CO₂[18,10,5]0.120.08Pale butterscotch glow
Earth-like N₂/O₂[8,14,32]0.180.12Deep blue Rayleigh scattering
Dense N₂[10,18,42]0.300.22Deep azure, dim stars lost
Dense CO₂[35,20,8]0.350.28Amber-orange haze
H₂/He (moon view)[12,15,22]0.100.06Pale steel-blue
Exotic/Silicate[30,12,15]0.450.35Ruddy volcanic haze

Extinction determines how many magnitudes of starlight the atmosphere absorbs, modulated by the density slider. At higher density, fainter stars disappear first — exactly as they do on a hazy night on Earth. Haze produces a horizon-hugging glow gradient that brightens where you look through the most atmosphere (lowest elevations), just as real atmospheric scattering works.

The atmospheric sky color is applied as a radial gradient (fainter at zenith, denser at horizon) plus a linear haze gradient concentrating near the ground plane. The density slider allows writers to explore the continuum from vacuum to soup, watching their sky evolve.

LAYER 6
Exoplanet Systems

Every observation point in EXOSKY is a real, confirmed exoplanet. Orbital parameters, distances, and host star properties come from the NASA Exoplanet Archive and associated discovery papers. The atmospheric compositions are informed by current modeling and theoretical constraints — marked as speculative where we're extrapolating.

CONFIDENCE LEVELS
Star position, distance, spectral typeMEASURED
Planet orbital parametersMEASURED
Planet mass / radiusMEASURED (most)
Atmospheric compositionMODELED / SPECULATIVE
Surface conditionsEXTRAPOLATED

We are transparent about what's known and what's inferred. The star catalogs are rock-solid. The atmospheric models are our best scientific guesses — and the density slider lets you explore the full range of possibilities.

RENDERING
Sky Projection

Stars are projected onto the screen using a gnomonic (central) projection — the same projection used in planetarium software. This maps the celestial sphere onto a flat tangent plane, preserving straight great-circle arcs as straight lines (important for constellation drawing).

The projection is centered on the observer's current viewing direction (RA, Dec) with a configurable field of view from 10° (narrow telescope view) to 160° (ultra-wide). Star positions are projected through rotation and central projection, with angular wrapping checks to prevent artifacts at field edges.

Star rendering uses a three-tier system: bright catalog stars get full radial-gradient glow and diffraction spikes; mid-brightness stars get simple filled circles; faint stars use fast sub-pixel fillRect() calls. This enables rendering ~20,000 simultaneous points at 60fps.

HONESTY
What We Don't Model (Yet)

Scientific integrity means saying what we can't do:

CURRENT LIMITATIONS
Interstellar reddeningNOT APPLIED TO INDIVIDUAL STARS
Axial precession (which star sits at the pole)MODELED (EPOCH slider)
Stellar proper motion (real star drift)NOT MODELED, NO PER-STAR DATA
Binary star systemsTREATED AS SINGLE POINTS
Nebulae / star clustersNOT RENDERED
Host star in local skyNOT RENDERED (sun position)
Light pollution / surface illuminationNOT MODELED
Zodiacal / galactic cirrusNOT MODELED

Individual stars do not have reddening applied based on distance and dust column, only the aggregate Milky Way band accounts for dust. The EPOCH slider (Layer 3C) rotates the named and procedural stars and the Milky Way band together the way real axial precession does, which changes which star sits near the pole over thousands of years, but it does not move any star relative to any other. It does not yet turn the 20,000-star background field, a separate static procedural texture rather than a set of individually tracked positions. Real stars also drift through space under their own proper motion, and that drift is not modeled here: this build has no per-star velocity data for the ~300 named stars or the exoplanet host stars, and fabricating motion vectors for real, named stars would be presenting invention as fact. The sky's shape (which stars are near which) is therefore a snapshot, even as the EPOCH slider turns which direction is "up."

FOR WRITERS
Using EXOSKY in Your Fiction

When you draw a constellation on an alien sky and name it, you're doing what every sapient species in the universe must do — finding patterns in the real. The stars your character sees from Kepler-442b are in the right places. The Milky Way is brighter and shifted because you're 1,100 light-years deeper into the Perseus Arm. The atmosphere dims and colors the sky because that's what CO₂ and N₂ actually do.

Your world's mythology can draw from a sky that's been honestly computed. The constellations your aliens name aren't arbitrary — they're the shapes that real stellar geometry produces from that specific vantage point. That's what makes them feel alive.

Every world exported from EXOSKY carries its provenance: the star coordinates, the viewing planet, the atmospheric model. Your world bible knows its own science.
