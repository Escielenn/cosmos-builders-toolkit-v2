ExoSky

The science · version 1.9

ExoSky shows the night sky from another star, then from the surface of a planet you decide: where its pole points, how fast it turns, where you stand on it, what time it is. This page says how each part is computed, what it is checked against, and what it leaves out. Every number below is tested in the code.

The stars

30,004 real stars, each with an id

The catalogue is a subset of the HYG database v4.1 (David Nash, astronexus; CC BY-SA 4.0), which merges Hipparcos, Yale Bright Star and Gliese. ExoSky keeps every star you can see with the naked eye from Earth (V ≤ 6.5, about 8,900 stars) plus every star within 100 parsecs of the Sun (about 24,700), loaded only when you open ExoSky.

Each star keeps its Hipparcos number (HIP 32349 is Sirius) or, without one, its HYG number. Constellations you draw store these ids, not names or positions, so they still point at the same stars after a correction.

One distance is overridden: Betelgeuse sits at 168 pc (Joyce et al. 2020) rather than the Hipparcos 152 pc. The override moves the catalogue star itself, so the point in the sky and the vantage agree.

// WHERE THE CATALOGUE THINS
The catalogue is built from Earth. An observer d parsecs from the Sun sees every neighbour within (100 − d) pc; beyond that, only stars bright enough to have been seen from Earth. From Kepler-452 (552 pc) faint nearby stars are missing, so the naked-eye count reads as a lower bound and the empty-sky warning stays quiet rather than mislead.

Moving the observer

Every star is translated by the observer's position and re-projected. Nothing is faked: a constellation changes shape because its stars sit at different distances.

direction = (star − observer) / |star − observer|
m = M + 5 log₁₀(d / 10 pc)

A star closer than 0.01 pc (about 2,000 AU) to the observer is the observer's own sun. It is drawn as a disc, not a point. (1.8 drew it as a phantom magnitude −26.7 point at right ascension 0°.)

Check: from Alpha Centauri A the Sun is a magnitude 0.4 star at right ascension 40°, declination +61°, in Cassiopeia, and Proxima Centauri is naked-eye. Both are in the tests.

The right way round

We see the sky from inside the sphere. Facing south, east is on your left, as on every star chart. ExoSky's camera takes screen-right as the view direction crossed with up; the orientation test puts Betelgeuse upper-left of Rigel and Sirius to their lower left from 40° north. (1.8 had this mirrored.)

The projection is stereographic: it keeps shapes and stays usable out to a 180° field. Zoom runs from 0.5° to 180°, by a constant factor per step.

Standing on a planet

Pole, equator, horizon

A planet's pole is a direction among the stars (right ascension and declination, J2000). Its equinox is where its orbit crosses its equator: pole × orbit normal. For Earth that is the March equinox exactly, which the tests check.

Obliquity fixes the angle between pole and orbit normal but not its direction. ExoSky uses a stated convention: the normal leans from the pole toward the north galactic pole. It decides which stars are behind the sun in which season, nothing else. A pole you have not set defaults to Earth's axis direction and is marked // POLE NOT SET.

zenith = cos φ (cos θ e₁ + sin θ e₂) + sin φ P

φ is your latitude and θ the local sidereal angle. The horizon is everything 90° from the zenith; altitude and azimuth follow.

Days

1 / solar day = 1 / sidereal day − 1 / year

Earth: 23.934 h sidereal gives a 24.000 h solar day. Venus, spinning backwards over 243.0 days, gets a 116.8-day solar day. Time of day sets where the sun stands: noon puts it on your meridian.

A tidally locked planet turns once per orbit, so its solar day is infinite. ExoSky pins the host star in the sky and replaces the time slider with your distance from the substellar point; moving through the orbit turns the stars instead.

The planet's own precession

The epoch slider precesses this planet's pole, not Earth's. Earth's measured period is 25,772 years (IAU 2006, Capitaine et al. 2003). For another world with no stated value, ExoSky estimates the solar torque alone:

rate = (3/2) (n² / ω) H cos ε

n is the orbital rate, ω the spin, ε the obliquity, H the dynamical ellipticity, scaled from Earth's (0.00327) by spin squared, as for a fluid body (Williams 1994). For Earth this gives about 80,000 years, the Sun-only figure; Earth's Moon brings the real value to 25,772. Moons are not included in the estimate, and the readout says so. A tidally locked world, with its spin on its orbit normal, has no precession to speak of.

The pole slides along a cone of half-angle ε, so a turn Δφ moves it by 2 asin(sin ε sin(Δφ/2)). When a constellation you drew at one epoch is viewed at another and the pole has moved more than 2°, ExoSky raises a Sky Drift flag citing both epochs.

Stars' own motions across the sky (proper motion) are not modelled. Over tens of thousands of years that matters for nearby stars; the slider says so.

The sun, the planets and the moons

R = √L (5772 K / T)² R☉
m_star(a) = M_V + 5 log₁₀(a / 10 pc)
m_body = m_star(r) − 2.5 log₁₀(p Φ(α) R² / Δ²)
Φ(α) = (sin α + (π − α) cos α) / π

The host's disc follows from its luminosity and temperature (Stefan-Boltzmann); its brightness from its V magnitude at the orbit's distance. Planets and moons shine by reflected light with a Lambert phase function. Checks in the tests: the Sun from Earth is 0.533° and magnitude −26.7; Jupiter at opposition about −2.7 (Mallama et al. 2018); the full Moon about −12.7; from TRAPPIST-1e, TRAPPIST-1f at closest approach is wider than our Moon.

Orbits are circular and share the planet's orbital plane. For Earth, positions start from the J2000 mean longitudes (Standish 1992) and are good to a few degrees; on 21 January 2000 the Moon comes out full, as it was (a total lunar eclipse that night). For TRAPPIST-1 and for planets in your world, starting phases are not known, so each body is placed deterministically by its id and the panel says so.

Host temperatures, luminosities and masses come from published values for every preset. A luminosity worked out from V alone misses most of a red dwarf's light, so it is used only for a star you place that has no published values.

Air and daylight

X = 1 / (cos z + 0.50572 (96.07995° − z)^−1.6364)
Δm = k X

Air mass X is Kasten and Young (1989): 1 overhead, 2 at 30° altitude, about 38 at the horizon. Extinction is k magnitudes per air mass. Earth's clear sky is k = 0.20. The other atmospheres scale it by column (0.06 for thin nitrogen up to 1.2 for silicate vapour); these are illustrative, not radiative transfer.

Daylight hides stars by raising the faintest magnitude you can see: about 6.5 with the sun 18° down, 2.5 at the end of civil twilight, near −4 at noon. This is a piecewise working figure, not a sky-brightness model. Sky colours are illustrative too: Rayleigh blue for nitrogen and oxygen, butterscotch for thin dusty carbon dioxide, amber for dense carbon dioxide.

The Milky Way

The band is ray-marched from the observer through a model galaxy: an exponential disc (scale length 2.6 kpc, height 300 pc), a bulge, a dust layer, and the seven spiral arms fitted by Reid et al. (2019) to maser parallaxes, including the Local (Orion) Arm the Sun sits beside. Each arm is a log-periodic spiral with a kink, ln(R / R_kink) = −(β − β_kink) tan ψ. Reid measured each arm only over certain azimuths; ExoSky extends them 60° further and marks readouts that fall on an extrapolated stretch.

The "nearest arm" readout comes from the same model. The band is a model rendered for its structure and direction, not a photograph.

Navigation and calendar

  • Pole star. The brightest star brighter than magnitude 4.5 within 5° of the pole (Polaris is 0.7° from Earth's). "None" is an answer: nothing to steer by.
  • Never sets. At latitude φ, the cap within |φ| of the visible pole: (1 − cos φ) / 2 of the sky.
  • Months. A moon's month from new to new: 1 / (1/P_moon − 1/P_year). Earth: 29.53 days.
  • Dawn rising. The first morning after its time lost in the sun's glare that the brightest star is seen rising. ExoSky uses a rule of thumb, labelled as such: the sun at least 10° below the horizon for a first-magnitude star, 1° more per magnitude fainter. Schaefer (1985, 1987) models this properly.

On the canon graph

ExoSky reads the planet, its star, its sibling planets and its moons from your world, and shows where each value came from: canon, Solaris, published values, the catalogue, derived, or not set. A value you set in ExoSky wins for the session; nothing is written until you choose to.

It can write: the pole (planet.pole_ra, planet.pole_dec), the precession period, the pole star and naked-eye count, where the system sits in the galaxy (star.galactic_position), and constellations. A constellation becomes an entity with its member star ids (constellation.stars[]), the epoch it was drawn in, and the planet it is seen from; the planet lists the names in planet.sky.constellations[] for Mythos. A write never overwrites canon silently: a different value already in canon is shown, and you keep it or replace it.

A system opened from Solaris has a star and an orbit but no place in the galaxy. ExoSky no longer invents one; it shows the sky from the Sun's neighbourhood and asks you to place the system at a real star or at galactic coordinates.

The scene brief lists conditions with the number each comes from. It never narrates and has no insert button; copy what you need by hand.

What ExoSky does not model

  • Proper motion: stars do not drift on the epoch slider.
  • Interstellar dust dimming individual stars (the band has dust; point stars do not).
  • Atmospheric refraction, light pollution, the observer's eyesight and dark adaptation.
  • Eccentric or inclined orbits, binary hosts' own orbits, star variability.
  • Moons' contribution to precession for any world but Earth.
