The Physics & Data Behind TIDELOCK
What Is Tidal Locking?

A tidally locked planet keeps one face permanently aimed at its star—the same way the Moon always shows the same face to Earth. One hemisphere bakes in eternal day. The other freezes in eternal night. Between them runs a permanent twilight band called the terminator.

This isn't exotic. It's the expected state for rocky planets orbiting M-dwarf and late K-dwarf stars within their habitable zones. Tidal locking timescales are inversely proportional to the sixth power of orbital distance—close planets lock fast. Since M-dwarf habitable zones are extremely close to the star, most potentially habitable worlds around these stars are tidally locked.

Pierrehumbert, R.T. (2011). "A Palette of Climates for Gliese 581g." The Astrophysical Journal Letters, 726(1), L8. DOI

Temperature Model

The surface temperature at any point on a tidally locked planet depends on its angular distance from the substellar point (the spot directly facing the star). TIDELOCK computes this using a modified Stefan-Boltzmann model with atmospheric redistribution:

T_SSP = (F_absorbed / σ)^0.25 × (1 + f_greenhouse × 0.55)

Where the absorbed flux is:

F = L★ × L☉ × (1 − albedo) / (16π × σ × d²)

The temperature gradient from substellar point (θ=0) to antistellar point (θ=π) follows:

Dayside: T(θ) = T_ASP + (T_SSP − T_ASP) × cos(θ)^n

where n = 1.0 − 0.7 × min(ρ_atm / 3, 1)

The exponent n controls how sharply temperature drops from the substellar point. A thicker atmosphere (higher ρ_atm) reduces n, spreading heat more evenly—modeling atmospheric heat transport from dayside to nightside.

The antistellar temperature is:

T_ASP = T_SSP × (0.1 + 0.4 × atm_normalized)

With no atmosphere, the nightside reaches near-absolute zero. With a dense atmosphere, heat redistribution raises the nightside temperature significantly—this is the key factor determining whether a tidally locked world can support a habitable terminator band.

Habitable Zone Calculation

Unlike conventional habitable zones (which are annular regions around a star), the habitable zone on a tidally locked planet is a band on the planet's surface—a ring around the terminator where temperatures fall between 273 K and 373 K (0°C to 100°C).

TIDELOCK scans the temperature profile from substellar to antistellar point at 3,600 angular steps, identifying the inner and outer edges of the habitable band:

Inner edge: T(θ_in) = 373 K (too hot inward)
Outer edge: T(θ_out) = 273 K (too cold outward)

Habitable band = θ_out − θ_in (in degrees)
Surface fraction ≈ |cos(θ_in) − cos(θ_out)| × 100%

Ocean coverage modulates the effective habitable area by up to 15%, accounting for oceanic thermal buffering. Worlds with >30° of habitable band are classified as "Habitable"; 5–30° as "Marginal"; below 5° as "Uninhabitable".

Kopparapu, R.K. et al. (2016). "The Inner Edge of the Habitable Zone for Synchronously Rotating Planets around Low-mass Stars Using General Circulation Models." The Astrophysical Journal, 819(1), 84. DOI
Atmosphere & Jeans Escape

A tidally locked planet's ability to retain its atmosphere is critical—without atmospheric heat transport, the terminator band collapses. TIDELOCK computes atmospheric retention using the Jeans escape parameter:

λ = (v_esc / v_thermal)²

v_esc = 11.186 × √(M_planet / R_planet) km/s
v_thermal = √(3 k_B T_SSP / m_molecule) / 1000 km/s

The Jeans parameter measures how strongly gravity binds atmospheric molecules. Higher λ means stronger retention:

Jeans ParameterRetentionImplication
λ > 25StrongAtmosphere stable over billions of years
10 < λ ≤ 25ModerateSlow loss; atmosphere persists for gigayears
6 < λ ≤ 10MarginalSignificant loss over geological time
λ ≤ 6LosingRapid escape; atmosphere stripped in megayears

The simulator calculates retention for N₂/O₂ (MW 29), CO₂ (44), H₂/He (4), CH₄ (16), and SO₂ (64). Different atmospheric compositions have very different retention properties—heavy molecules like CO₂ and SO₂ are retained far more easily than light hydrogen.

Wind & Atmospheric Transport

The extreme temperature difference between the dayside and nightside drives powerful atmospheric circulation. TIDELOCK estimates maximum wind velocities using:

v_max = min(√ΔT × 15 × √(ρ_atm + 0.1), 500) m/s

Where ΔT = T_SSP − T_ASP. Atmospheric scale height—how rapidly pressure decreases with altitude—is computed from thermodynamic first principles:

H = k_B × T_term / (m_molecule × g) km

These values determine whether the atmosphere can effectively transport heat, maintain cloud systems, and sustain weather patterns at the terminator.

Orbital Mechanics

Orbital periods follow Kepler's third law directly:

P = √(a³ / M★) years = √(a³ / M★) × 365.25 days

Where a is the semi-major axis in AU and M★ is the stellar mass in solar masses. For a planet at M4V's habitable zone (0.074 AU), the orbital period is approximately 8.7 days—the planet's "year" is just over a week.

Tidal locking is enforced by maintaining the planet's local +X axis pointed toward the star at all times, using quaternion rotation. The terminator band remains perpendicular to the star direction as the planet orbits.

Terrain & Biome Rendering

Surface terrain is generated using fractal Brownian motion (fBM)—five octaves of 3D Simplex noise layered at decreasing amplitudes. The terrain responds to the tidal environment:

Substellar region: Volcanic caldera features from intense stellar heating. Mountain amplitude is reduced—thermal expansion fractures prevent large mountain building.

Terminator band: Enhanced mountain ridges from thermal stress at the day-night boundary. These ridges trap moisture from dayside evaporation, creating rivers and lakes in sheltered valleys.

Nightside: Smooth, ancient ice plains. Reduced geological activity produces gently rolling terrain under permanent ice sheets.

Biomes are determined procedurally from local temperature, elevation, and moisture:

Cloud Layer & Moon System

Clouds are rendered as a procedural shell at 1.012× planet radius using animated fractal noise. Cloud density concentrates at the terminator—where warm dayside air meets cold nightside air, driving convection and precipitation. Cloud opacity scales with atmospheric density, capped at 35%.

Moons (up to 3) orbit the planet with independent orbital distances and speeds. Moon eclipse shadows are computed in the fragment shader by projecting each moon's position along the star direction vector and applying a smooth falloff from full shadow at the moon center to no shadow at 1.3× moon radius.

Computed Planet Physics

The data readout panel calculates over 20 physical quantities in real-time from the input parameters:

Surface gravity: g = (M / R²) × 9.81 m/s²
Escape velocity: v_esc = 11.186 × √(M / R) km/s
Bulk density: ρ = 5.51 × (M / R³) g/cm³
Orbital period: P = √(a³ / M★) × 365.25 days

All values use Earth-normalized units: mass in M⊕, radius in R⊕, distance in AU. Reference constants: g_Earth = 9.81 m/s², v_esc_Earth = 11.186 km/s, ρ_Earth = 5.51 g/cm³.

What This Tool Cannot Do

No atmospheric GCM. The temperature model uses an analytic gradient, not a full general circulation model. Real atmospheric dynamics involve Rossby waves, Hadley cells, and complex cloud feedback that require supercomputer simulations to resolve.

No stellar activity. M-dwarf flares, coronal mass ejections, and XUV radiation—which can strip atmospheres and sterilize surfaces—are not modeled. These are critical habitability factors for real exoplanets.

No ocean dynamics. Ocean heat transport, ice-albedo feedback, and subsurface ocean circulation are not simulated. Real tidally locked oceans would redistribute heat significantly.

No plate tectonics. The terrain model is procedural, not geophysically driven. Real tidal heating and mantle convection patterns on tidally locked worlds would produce different geological features.

No atmospheric chemistry. Photochemical reactions, ozone layers, and biosignature gases are not modeled.

Despite these limitations, TIDELOCK produces physically grounded visualizations. The temperature profiles, habitable zone geometry, biome distributions, and atmospheric retention calculations follow established exoplanet science. When you build a world here, you're building on real physics.
For Writers

TIDELOCK exists to help science fiction writers explore a specific question: what does the surface of a tidally locked world actually look like?

The "eyeball Earth" concept—a frozen world with a single liquid ocean facing the star—is just one possibility. Adjust the atmosphere and you get a world with a wide habitable belt, permanent twilight civilizations along the terminator, and weather systems driven by eternal day-night temperature gradients. The scenarios TIDELOCK reveals—terminator forests, substellar deserts, nightside ice caps, ribbon cities along the habitable band—emerge from the physics, not from artistic license.

TIDELOCK is part of the StellarForge worldbuilding toolkit. The Star System Builder and Planetary Profile worksheets can help you develop the worlds you discover here into full settings—following the cascading principle that environment shapes biology, biology shapes psychology, psychology shapes culture, and culture shapes mythology.
