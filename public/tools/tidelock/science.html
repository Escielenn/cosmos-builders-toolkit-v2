Showing Our Work

The physics and data behind Tidelock
What is tidal locking?

A tidally locked planet keeps one face aimed at its star, the way the Moon keeps one face toward Earth. One hemisphere has permanent day, the other permanent night. Rocky planets close to small stars lock quickly: the despinning time scales with the sixth power of orbital distance.

Tidelock does not assume a world is locked. It computes the locking time and compares it with the age you set for the system (see Locking below). If the world would still be turning, Tidelock says so.

Pierrehumbert, R.T. (2011). "A Palette of Climates for Gliese 581g." ApJ Letters, 726, L8.
One set of formulas

Every number Tidelock shows comes from /tools/_shared/physics.js, the same module (twin of src/lib/physics) that the rest of StellarForge uses. A unit test loads both and fails if they ever disagree, and another checks them against Earth, Mars, Proxima b and TRAPPIST-1e.

Light and orbit
S / S⊕ = (L / L☉) / a²    P = 365.25 × √(a³ / (M★ + m_p)) days

Insolation S is in units of the sunlight Earth receives (1361 W/m²). The orbital period uses Kepler's third law with the star's mass. Locked, the planet turns once per orbit.

Surface temperature

The temperature at an angle θ from the substellar point comes from one energy-balance profile:

σT(θ)⁴ = S·S⊕·(1 − A) · [ (1 − ε)·max(cos θ, 0) + (ε/4)·(1 + (1 − ε)·cos θ) ] · (1 + ¾τ) + σT_int⁴

A is the Bond albedo. ε is the share of absorbed starlight the atmosphere carries away from where it lands (0 = none, 1 = perfectly even). τ is the infrared optical depth of a grey greenhouse (Eddington approximation): Earth's 255 K → 288 K needs τ ≈ 0.84. T_int = 35 K is the floor from internal heat at Earth's heat flow, which is what an airless permanent night settles to.

With no air (ε = 0, τ = 0) the substellar point reaches T₀(1 − A)^¼, where T₀ = (S·S⊕/σ)^¼ is 394 K at Earth's distance from the Sun. Tidelock 1.8 used the planet-average form (the 1/4 factor), which is √2 too cold for a bare day side. The readout shows both the bare-rock substellar temperature and the surface with your atmosphere.

The profile is continuous through the terminator, so the day side, the terminator and the night side come from the same equation. The (1 − ε)·cos θ term tilts the carried heat toward the day side when transport is weak, so the night side cools away from the terminator to a cold antistellar point, as tidally locked climate models show. That tilt is a StellarForge interpolation rather than a published fit; it integrates to zero over the sphere, so energy is conserved.

Cowan, N.B. & Agol, E. (2011). "The Statistics of Albedo and Heat Recirculation on Hot Exoplanets." ApJ, 729, 54. Catling, D.C. & Kasting, J.F. (2017). Atmospheric Evolution on Inhabited and Lifeless Worlds, §2. Davies, J.H. & Davies, D.R. (2010). Solid Earth, 1, 5.
Heat transport depends on pressure

How much heat reaches the night side depends on how thick the atmosphere is. Tidelock compares the time the air takes to radiate its heat away with the time winds take to carry it across the planet:

τ_rad = (p / g) · c_p / (4σT³)    τ_adv = R_p / U    ε = τ_rad / (τ_rad + τ_adv)

U = 20 m/s, a typical terminator-crossing wind in rocky tidally locked climate models. An Earth-like 1 bar atmosphere gives ε ≈ 0.9 (mild night side); 0.01 bar gives ε ≈ 0.08 (scorched day, frozen night).

Showman, A.P. & Guillot, T. (2002). A&A, 385, 166. Koll, D.D.B. & Abbot, D.S. (2016). "Temperature Structure and Atmospheric Circulation of Dry Tidally Locked Rocky Exoplanets." ApJ, 825, 99.
Atmosphere collapse

If the coldest point of the night side is below the temperature at which the main gas condenses at the surface pressure, the atmosphere freezes out onto the night side. Tidelock flags this, and says the temperatures shown assume an atmosphere that would not survive. Condensation points follow the Clausius–Clapeyron relation from NIST reference values: N₂ 77.4 K, CO₂ 194.7 K (sublimation), CH₄ 111.7 K, SO₂ 263.1 K, H₂ 20.3 K, each at 1 atm.

Joshi, M.M., Haberle, R.M. & Reynolds, R.T. (1997). "Simulations of the Atmospheres of Synchronously Rotating Terrestrial Planets Orbiting M Dwarfs." Icarus, 129, 450. Wordsworth, R. (2015). ApJ, 806, 180.
The liquid-water band

Water is liquid between 273.15 K and its boiling point, which falls with pressure (373 K at 1 atm, about 319 K at 0.1 bar). Below the triple point (0.006 bar) it cannot be liquid at all. Tidelock scans the profile in 0.1° steps and reports the band's inner and outer angles, its width in degrees of arc and in kilometres (arc × planet radius), and the fraction of the surface it covers, (cos θ_in − cos θ_out) / 2.

With thick air the band can cover most of the day side and need not sit at the terminator. With thin air it narrows to a ring near the substellar point or vanishes. Ocean coverage changes the picture's colours, not the band.

Locking
t_lock = ω a⁶ I Q / (3 G M★² k₂ R⁵)    I = 0.4 m R²    k₂ = 1.5 / (1 + 19μ / (2ρgR))

Initial spin 12 hours, tidal quality factor Q = 100, rigidity μ = 3 × 10¹⁰ N/m². Earth around the Sun would take ~10¹¹ years; Proxima b about 10⁴ to 10⁵. If the locking time is under a tenth of the system's age the world reads Locked; under the age, Likely locked (Q is uncertain by about a factor of ten); otherwise Not locked, and the tool says its day-and-night picture does not apply.

Gladman, B. et al. (1996). "Synchronous Locking of Tidally Evolving Satellites." Icarus, 122, 166. Peale, S.J. (1977), in Planetary Satellites.
Gas retention

A gas stays for billions of years if its molecules' mean thermal speed is at most a sixth of the escape velocity, and leaks away fast above a fifth:

v̄ = √(8kT / πm)    v_esc = 11.186 × √(M / R) km/s    retained if v̄ ≤ v_esc / 6

Tidelock tests the lightest bulk gas of the chosen composition with its real molecular mass (H₂ 2.016, CH₄ 16.04, N₂ 28.01, CO₂ 44.01, SO₂ 64.07 g/mol) at the top of the atmosphere, where escape happens. That exobase temperature is assumed: 1000 K for Earth-like and hydrogen atmospheres, 300 K where CO₂ or SO₂ cools the upper air, as on Venus and Mars. Stellar winds and EUV, strong around M dwarfs, strip more than this thermal estimate.

Seager, S. (2010). Exoplanet Atmospheres, Princeton, ch. 4. Bougher, S.W. et al. (2015), in Mars Upper Atmosphere.
Other readouts
g = M / R² (Earth units)    ρ = 5.514 × M / R³ g/cm³    H = kT / (μ m_u g)    t_MS ≈ 10 Gyr × M / L

Scale height H uses the terminator temperature and the composition's mean molecular mass. The star's main-sequence lifetime is an order-of-magnitude estimate; if the system age exceeds it, a flag says the star would already have swollen into a giant.

Terrain, clouds and moons

The terrain is procedural decoration (fractal noise), not a geological model, and the clouds are a drifting noise layer. Moons cast eclipse shadows but have no tidal effect on the planet. None of these change the numbers. Cloud drift, moons and the orbit animation stop when your system asks for reduced motion.

What this tool cannot do

No climate model. The profile is an energy balance with one heat-transport number, not a general circulation model. Real atmospheres form jets, substellar cloud decks and cold traps that shift temperatures by tens of kelvin.

No stellar activity. Flares, coronal mass ejections and XUV stripping, critical around M dwarfs, are outside the thermal retention test.

No oceans or ice feedback. Ocean heat transport and ice-albedo feedback are not simulated; albedo is the value you set.

Every formula above is in public/tools/_shared/physics.js with its source in a comment. Where Tidelock assumes a value (wind speed, exobase temperature, tidal Q), the readout or this page says so.
For writers

Read three numbers before placing anyone on a locked world: the liquid-water band in kilometres, the night-side frost point, and the time to lock. The Conditions brief button on the simulator page turns the current numbers into a short, cited list of what is physically true there. It is a field manual, not prose: it never writes into your manuscript.

Tidelock is part of the StellarForge toolkit. Publish a planet to your world and its band width, temperatures and lock state become facts the Studio can cite.
