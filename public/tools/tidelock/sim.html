TIDELOCK

Most potentially habitable exoplanets orbit red dwarf stars — and most of those are tidally locked, with one face permanently baking in starlight and the other in eternal darkness.

The substellar point, directly beneath the star, gets the full force of the light. The antistellar point never sees it. Whether the night side freezes, or the atmosphere carries enough heat round to keep it mild, depends on how thick that atmosphere is: thin air leaves a scorched day and a frozen night, thick air evens them out. Somewhere on the surface, water can be liquid; Tidelock finds that band and measures it in kilometres.

Drag to orbit the planet in 3D. Scroll to zoom — zoom far out to see the full orbital system with habitable zone. Add moons to see eclipse shadows sweep across the surface.

What the Colors Mean

  • Beige/rust: Dayside — scorched rocky surface, too hot for life
  • Green: Habitable zone — where life could exist, liquid water is stable
  • Blue: Oceans — liquid water in low-elevation habitable areas
  • White: Nightside ice — permanently frozen

Terrain

The terrain is procedural decoration, not a geological model: rougher near the terminator, smoother ice on the night side. The temperatures, band and flags come from the physics, not the terrain.

Key insight for writers

With a thin atmosphere the liquid-water band is a narrow ring near the terminator. With a thick one it can widen to cover most of the day side, and it need not sit at the terminator at all. Check the band width and the night-side frost point before placing a civilization. If the night side is below the frost point of the air, the air itself freezes out there.
