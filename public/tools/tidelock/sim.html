TIDELOCK

Most potentially habitable exoplanets orbit red dwarf stars — and most of those are tidally locked, with one face permanently baking in starlight and the other in eternal darkness.

The substellar point — directly beneath the star — is scorched rock, a barren Mercury-like wasteland. The antistellar point is frozen white ice, colder than any place on Earth. Between these extremes lies the terminator band: a ring of permanent twilight where liquid water, breathable air, and life itself might exist.

Drag to orbit the planet in 3D. Scroll to zoom — zoom far out to see the full orbital system with habitable zone. Add moons to see eclipse shadows sweep across the surface.

What the Colors Mean

  • Beige/rust: Dayside — scorched rocky surface, too hot for life
  • Green: Habitable zone — where life could exist, liquid water is stable
  • Blue: Oceans — liquid water in low-elevation habitable areas
  • White: Nightside ice — permanently frozen

Tectonics

Tidal locking creates predictable geology: a volcanic caldera at the substellar point, massive mountain ranges along the terminator where thermal expansion meets cold contraction, and smooth ancient ice plains on the nightside. Mountains trap moisture, creating rivers and lakes in the habitable band.

Key Insight for Writers

The habitable band isn't a thin line — with a decent atmosphere, it can be thousands of kilometers wide. Civilizations here live in permanent twilight, in ribbon-shaped nations stretched along the terminator. The star is forever on one horizon, the stars forever on the other.
