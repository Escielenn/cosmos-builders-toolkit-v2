Showing Our Work

The Physics & Data Behind ROGUE
Gravitational Physics

ROGUE is a full N-body gravitational simulator—every object in the simulation exerts gravitational force on every other object simultaneously. There are no shortcuts, rails, or predetermined paths. Planetary orbits emerge naturally from Newton's law of universal gravitation:

F = G · m₁ · m₂ / r²

We work in solar-normalized units: distances in Astronomical Units (AU), masses in solar masses (M☉), and time in years. In these units, the gravitational constant simplifies elegantly:

G = 4π² AU³ · yr⁻² · M☉⁻¹

This is not an approximation—it's an exact consequence of Kepler's third law. A 1 M☉ star at 1 AU produces an orbital period of exactly 1 year.

Numerical Integrator

Orbital mechanics is solved using a kick-drift-kick leapfrog integrator, a second-order symplectic method that conserves energy far better than naive Euler integration. Each timestep proceeds in three phases:

1. Half-kick: v += a · dt/2
2. Drift: x += v · dt
3. Recompute forces at new positions
4. Half-kick: v += a_new · dt/2

Symplectic integrators are preferred for orbital mechanics because they don't artificially inject or drain energy from the system over long timescales—orbits stay stable for thousands of revolutions rather than spiraling inward or outward.

Leapfrog integration: Hut, P., Makino, J., & McMillan, S. (1995). "Building a better leapfrog." The Astrophysical Journal, 443, L93–L96. ADS

A gravitational softening parameter ε² = 5×10⁻⁵ AU² prevents numerical singularities during very close encounters, replacing 1/r² with 1/(r² + ε²). This is standard practice in N-body simulation and represents the fact that real bodies have finite size.

Softening in N-body simulations: Dehnen, W. (2001). "Towards optimal softening in three-dimensional N-body codes." Monthly Notices of the Royal Astronomical Society, 324(2), 273–291. DOI
Conditional N-Body Forces

Before the intruder is launched, planet-to-planet gravitational forces are intentionally disabled. Only star↔planet forces are computed. This keeps planets in perfect Keplerian orbits during the "before" state.

Why? Tightly-packed systems like TRAPPIST-1 are stabilized in reality by precise mean-motion resonance chains—delicate integer orbital period ratios maintained over billions of years. A simple N-body integrator cannot maintain these resonances; within seconds of simulation time, accumulated numerical errors break the resonance locks and the system flies apart. This is not a failure of our physics—it's a known limitation of direct integration without resonance-aware corrections.

Once the intruder launches, full N-body forces activate. The gravitational disruption from the intruder overwhelms any resonance effects anyway, so the full force computation produces physically meaningful results.

TRAPPIST-1 resonance chain: Luger, R. et al. (2017). "A seven-planet resonant chain in TRAPPIST-1." Nature Astronomy, 1, 0129. DOI
Adaptive Timestep

Different systems require vastly different temporal resolution. TRAPPIST-1's innermost planet orbits in 1.5 Earth days; Neptune takes 165 years. A single timestep size cannot serve both.

The timestep scales with the innermost orbital radius of the active system:

dt = dt_base × min(1, r_min / 0.5 AU)
steps/frame = 60 × timeScale × min(80, 1/dtScale)

This ensures compact systems get finer temporal resolution while wall-clock simulation speed remains visually consistent. The step count per frame is capped at 8,000 to prevent browser freezes.

Habitable Zones

The green habitable zone overlay shows the conservative habitable zone—the range of distances where liquid water could exist on a rocky planet's surface, assuming an Earth-like atmosphere. These boundaries are computed from stellar luminosity using the models of Kopparapu et al. (2013):

HZ_inner = √(L / S_eff,inner)  HZ_outer = √(L / S_eff,outer)

Where L is stellar luminosity and S_eff is the effective stellar flux at each boundary. For main-sequence stars, luminosity scales approximately as L ∝ M^3.5.

Kopparapu, R.K. et al. (2013). "Habitable zones around main-sequence stars: New estimates." The Astrophysical Journal, 765(2), 131. DOI

Velocity & Unit Conversions

Velocities displayed in the data panel are converted from simulation units (AU/yr) to km/s using:

1 AU/yr ≈ 4.7405 km/s

Orbital velocities for circular orbits are initialized using the vis-viva equation at zero eccentricity:

v = √(G · M_central / a)

Where a is the semi-major axis and M_central is the total mass interior to the orbit (star + binary companion if applicable).

Visual Sizing

Bodies are not drawn to physical scale—at true scale, every planet would be invisible at every zoom level. Instead, we use a proportional sizing system tied to stellar radius:

Star: max(6, min(28, zoom × 0.04 × M^0.8 + 4)) px
Planet: max(1.8, min(star × 0.45, zoom × 0.012 × size + 1.2)) px

The M^0.8 exponent approximates the main-sequence mass-radius relation for stars. The 45% cap ensures planets never visually rival their host star.

Main-sequence mass-radius relation: Demircan, O. & Kahraman, G. (1991). "Stellar mass-luminosity and mass-radius relations." Astrophysics and Space Science, 181, 313–322. DOI
What This Tool Cannot Do

ROGUE is designed for gravitational dynamics visualization, not astrophysical precision. Important limitations:

No general relativity. All physics is Newtonian. This means no perihelion precession, no frame-dragging near black holes, and no gravitational waves. For the stellar-distance encounters ROGUE simulates, Newtonian gravity is an excellent approximation.

No tidal effects. Bodies are point masses. There is no Roche limit disruption, no tidal heating, no tidal locking evolution.

No collisions. Bodies pass through each other at close approach. The softening parameter prevents numerical instability but does not represent real physics at very close range.

No resonance maintenance. As noted above, the integrator cannot sustain the delicate mean-motion resonances that stabilize real multi-planet systems over geological timescales.

No atmospheric or magnetic effects. The simulation is purely gravitational—no radiation pressure, stellar wind, or electromagnetic interactions.

Despite these limitations, the gravitational dynamics shown are physically meaningful. The trajectories, ejections, orbit swaps, and captures you see are genuine solutions to the N-body problem under Newtonian gravity, computed in real-time from first principles.
For Writers

ROGUE exists to help science fiction writers explore a specific question: what happens to a planetary system when something massive passes through it?

The scenarios you discover here—planets ejected into interstellar space, orbits destabilized into ellipses, moons captured by intruders, habitable worlds flung into the dark—are not arbitrary. They emerge from the same mathematics that governs real stellar encounters. When you write these events into your fiction, you're writing physics.

ROGUE is part of the StellarForge worldbuilding toolkit. The Star System and Planetary Design worksheets can help you develop the worlds you discover here into full settings with geology, atmospheres, biospheres, and cultures—all following the cascading principle that environment shapes biology, biology shapes psychology, psychology shapes culture, and culture shapes mythology.
